Animation

Frames and keyframes

A keyframe is a frame where the artwork changes. Ordinary frames after it hold that artwork on screen. Frame-by-frame animation is a keyframe on every frame or two; tweened animation is keyframes further apart with Phoenix filling in between.

Insert and remove frames

CommandShortcutDoes
FrameF5Adds frames, extending how long the current artwork holds
KeyframeF6Makes a keyframe that starts as a copy of the previous one
Blank KeyframeF7Makes an empty keyframe, to draw the next drawing from scratch
Remove FramesShift+F5Deletes the selected frames, closing the gap
Clear KeyframeShift+F6Turns a keyframe back into an ordinary frame
Clear FramesAlt+BackspaceEmpties the selected frames, leaving the frames after them where they are

Frame, Keyframe and Blank Keyframe are in Insert › Timeline; the others are in Edit › Timeline. All but Clear Frames are also on the menu you get by right-clicking frames. Each acts on every selected frame, or on the playhead's frame of the current layer.

Tip

You don't always need F6. If you draw on a frame of a layer that is only holding an earlier keyframe, Phoenix makes the keyframe for you.

Select frames

  • Click a cell to select it. This also moves the playhead there and makes that layer current.
  • Drag across cells, including across several layers, to select a block.
  • Shift-click to extend the selection.
  • Ctrl-click to add single frames anywhere, on any layer, or take them out again. Frame commands such as F6 or Clear Keyframe then act on each of them.
  • Edit › Timeline › Select All Frames (Ctrl+Alt+A) selects every frame of every layer.

To select whole spans between keyframes with one click, turn on Span Based Selection in the frame view menu.

Move, copy and stretch frames

Frame commands

Cut, Copy, Paste Frames
Ctrl+Alt+X, C and V. Paste Frames inserts, pushing later frames along.
Paste and Overwrite Frames
Pastes over the frames already there instead of inserting.
Reverse Frames
Plays the selected frames backward.
Convert to Frame-by-Frame Animation
Turns a tween into a keyframe on every frame, ready to adjust by hand.
Copy Motion, Paste Motion
Copies a motion tween's movement and gives it to another symbol instance or text block, alone on its keyframe, as a motion tween of its own starting from where that object is.
Remove Tween
Removes the classic, shape or motion tween from the selected spans, leaving their keyframes. A span that had a motion tween holds its object where the motion started.
Distribute to Keyframes
In Modify › Timeline. Select several objects on one frame and each goes on a keyframe of its own, one after another: the bottom one stays, the next goes on the frame after, and so on.

Edit Multiple Frames

Turn on Edit › Timeline › Edit Multiple Frames to see the keyframes within the onion skin markers in full, not faded, and to select and change their artwork together: drag a marquee over a whole run of drawings and move them all at once, for example. Hidden and locked layers are left out. Set the range by dragging the onion skin markers in the ruler.

Frame labels

Click a keyframe and use the Label section of the Frame tab to name it. The Type list makes it a Name (a marker scripts can jump to), a Comment (a note for yourself), or an Anchor. Labels show in the timeline, which makes long scenes easier to find your way around.

These are in the Control menu, and the timeline header has buttons for the first frame, stepping, play, onion skin and loop. They work when the stage or timeline has focus, so they never interfere with typing in a text box or panel.

Playback runs at the document's frame rate. If a frame takes too long to draw, Phoenix skips ahead to stay in time, so what you see matches the real speed. Sounds play along, unless you turn on View › Mute Sounds (Ctrl+Alt+M).

Looping a range

Turn on Loop Playback and drag the ends of the loop range in the ruler to play just part of the timeline over and over, which is ideal for polishing a cycle.

Write scripts: the Actions panel

Window › Actions (F9) shows the script of the keyframe at the playhead on the current layer, ready to type into. Select a button or movie clip instance on the stage instead and it shows that instance's own script: its on (release) and onClipEvent handlers, in ActionScript 1 and 2.

  • What you type goes into the document as you type. Each script you work on is one step in the undo history until you move to another script or click away, so Ctrl+Z on the stage takes back the whole edit. Inside the editor, Ctrl+Z undoes typing.
  • The script navigator on the left lists every script in the document, scene by scene and symbol by symbol. Click one to edit it without moving the playhead. The ☰ button shows or hides it.
  • Pin keeps the script in the panel while you select other things and move the playhead. Picking a script in the navigator pins it; click Pin again to follow the selection.
  • Check checks the script's syntax. It is also checked each time it goes into the document: the line with an error is marked, and the line below the editor says what is wrong.
  • Find (Ctrl+F) finds, and Ctrl+H replaces. Ctrl+G goes to a line, and Ctrl+/ comments the selected lines out or back in.
  • Tab and Shift+Tab indent and outdent the selected lines. A new line keeps the indent, one step deeper after an opening brace.
  • Target (Insert Target Path) lists the named movie clips and buttons the script can reach, those inside them too, and types the path to the one you pick at the caret: this.hat.bow, or from the root (_root.hat.bow) for a script on a scene's timeline. Only instances with a name are listed; name them in the Properties panel.

To play what you wrote, use Test Movie.

Frame scripts

Frames can carry scripts, and files made for broadcast often use a few of them to hold a pose or repeat a section. Turn on Control › Enable Simple Frame Actions (Ctrl+Alt+F) and playback follows these calls in the timeline's frame scripts:

stop()
Playback stops on that frame.
play()
Playback carries on, after a stop() earlier in the same script.
gotoAndPlay(10), gotoAndPlay("walk")
Jumps to a frame number (counting from 1, as the ruler does) or to a frame label, and plays on from there.
gotoAndStop(...)
Jumps to the frame or label and stops there.
nextFrame(), prevFrame()
Steps one frame on or back and stops.

The calls can be written with this. in front. A script runs when the playhead arrives at its keyframe, but not the frame you start from, so pressing Enter on a frame with stop() plays on past it. Playback on the stage does not run the scripts: anything else in them is ignored, and so is a call it cannot be sure of, such as one inside an if, a function or a block, one aimed at another clip, or a goto to a computed frame or another scene. Scripts on guide layers are left out. To run a movie's scripts in full, use Test Movie.

The setting is off by default and is remembered. While it is on, movie clips follow the same calls in their own frame scripts, both as they play on the stage during playback and in exported movies, so a clip with stop() on its first frame holds that frame instead of looping.

Test Movie

Control › Test Movie (Ctrl+Enter) plays the movie in a window of its own as Flash Player would, at the document's frame rate, running its ActionScript. It starts at the first scene and plays the scenes in turn, back to the first after the last. Buttons light up under the mouse and answer clicks, and keys typed in the window go to the movie, so an interactive movie, such as a dress-up game, can be played. Restart plays it again from the first frame; Pause holds it, and Step moves it on one frame at a time. Close the window to go back to editing; the document is not changed by playing it.

Scripts in ActionScript 1 and 2, as older movies and games are written, run as Flash Player runs them:

  • Frame scripts run as their keyframes are reached, on the root timeline and in every movie clip, after the frame's objects are placed.
  • stop(), play(), gotoAndPlay(), gotoAndStop(), nextFrame() and prevFrame(), on the timeline or on a named clip (hat.gotoAndStop(3)), and tellTarget.
  • nextScene() and prevScene(), and gotos that name a scene (gotoAndPlay("Ending", 1)) or a label in another scene.
  • on (release), on (press), on (rollOver) and the other button events, on (keyPress "<Left>"), and onClipEvent (load), onClipEvent (enterFrame), onClipEvent (keyDown) and onClipEvent (keyUp) on movie clips.
  • The keyboard: Key.isDown(Key.LEFT), Key.getCode(), Key.getAscii() and Key.addListener().
  • A clip's _x, _y, _visible, _alpha, _xscale, _yscale, _rotation, _currentframe and _totalframes, read and set by dot (hat._visible = false) or with getProperty and setProperty, and clips found by name or by path ("/hat", _root.hat, _parent).
  • Variables, if, loops, functions, ifFrameLoaded (everything counts as loaded) and trace(), whose output goes to the Output panel (F2).

Frame scripts in ActionScript 3 run too, with what simple games use:

  • addEventListener and removeEventListener on named movie clips and buttons and on stage, for MouseEvent.CLICK, MOUSE_DOWN, MOUSE_UP, ROLL_OVER, ROLL_OUT, MOUSE_OVER and MOUSE_OUT, Event.ENTER_FRAME, and KeyboardEvent.KEY_DOWN and KEY_UP (with keyCode). Mouse events go on up to the clips a clip is in. A movie clip with a mouse listener answers clicks as a button does.
  • A clip's x, y, visible, alpha, scaleX, scaleY, rotation, currentFrame, totalFrames, currentLabel, name, parent and root, MovieClip(root), and import lines, which are taken as read.
  • Movie clip symbols exported for ActionScript with a class name: new Hat() makes one, addChild puts it on the stage and removeChild takes it off.

And in both, what games most often do:

  • Text fields. A dynamic text field with an instance name is reached by it, and score_txt.text = "Score: " + score changes what it shows, in its own font and color. A field that names a variable (ActionScript 1 and 2) shows the variable's value as it changes.
  • Dragging. startDrag() and stopDrag(), with a lock to the center and bounds, as a function or on a clip; _droptarget (or dropTarget) says where it was let go.
  • Hit tests. hitTest(x, y, shapeFlag) and hitTest(otherClip), and hitTestPoint and hitTestObject; with the shape flag, the clip's art counts rather than its box. Buttons and clips are clicked by their art too, so a click between two buttons finds neither.
  • The mouse. _xmouse and _ymouse (mouseX and mouseY) in a clip's own space, and onClipEvent (mouseDown), (mouseUp) and (mouseMove).
  • Clips from the library. attachMovie("linkage", "name", depth, {_x: 10}), duplicateMovieClip() and removeMovieClip(), for symbols exported for ActionScript.
  • Sound. Library sounds exported for ActionScript: new Sound() with attachSound("linkage"), start(seconds, loops), stop() and setVolume(), or a sound's class with play(); stopAllSounds() and SoundMixer.stopAll().
  • getTimer() counts the milliseconds since the movie started.

A script error stops that script only, and is shown in the Output panel with the frame or button and the line it was on; the movie plays on. Double-click the error, once the Test Movie window is closed, to see the script in the Actions panel with the caret on that line. getURL() opens web links in your browser.

Onion skinning

Onion skinning shows nearby frames faintly behind the current one, so you can draw each new drawing in relation to the last. Turn it on with the onion skin button in the timeline header, or View › Onion Skin (Alt+Shift+O).

Drag the markers in the ruler to set how many frames are shown, or choose a preset and other options from View › Onion Skin Settings (also by right-clicking the onion skin button):

Onion 1, 2, 5, All
How many frames to show on each side.
Outline
Shows the other frames as outlines only.
Keyframes Only
Skips held frames and shows only drawings.
Anchor Onion
Keeps the onion skin on the frames it covers when you turn this on, instead of moving with the playhead: step through a run of frames and compare each with the same few. Drag the markers in the ruler to change which frames it stays on.
Opacity
25, 45, 65 or 85%.
Color Before, Color After
The tints for earlier and later frames.

Locked layers are left out of onion skinning, so lock the background to keep it from cluttering the view.
